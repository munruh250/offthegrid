01–03

Layout, without panels

The default habit is a floating card with a hairline border and a pill tag. All three of these refuse that. Instead they borrow from things that already exist in this world: a survey sheet, a field instrument, a broadcast overlay.

04–08

Map treatments

Terrain is generated from fractal noise, not drawn as tiles — so contours, coastline and ridgelines are continuous and the hex grid sits on top of nature rather than replacing it. Each treatment pushes the grid back by a different amount.

The grid should be an instrument, not the terrain. Civilization draws the world as hexes; here the world is continuous and the hex is a survey overlay laid over it — the abstraction the player uses to reason about a place, not the place itself. That framing lets the grid fade almost to nothing during exploration and firm up during planning, without the map ever looking like a board game.

What changed structurally. No rounded corners, no bordered cards, no pill tags. Type does the separating instead of boxes: extreme weight and size contrast, ragged asymmetric alignment, elements bleeding off the frame, leader lines connecting data to the thing it describes. Grain over everything.

On the ruler. The day control is drawn as a measuring rule rather than a slider — small thing, but it's the sort of detail that decides whether an interface feels authored or assembled.

Still to resolve. Hex size against thumb reach: at 300px wide, a 26px hex is below the 44px tap target, so selection needs either a larger grid or tap-nearest-centre with a generous radius. Worth testing on a real device before the grid size is locked.
